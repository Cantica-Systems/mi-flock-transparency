Refreshed once a day

A plate read here is searchable from many states.

Every searchthe ledger has read stays on the record.

Police departments that use Flock Safety licence plate cameras each publish a transparency portal. It says how many cameras the department runs, lists every search its officers ran, and names the outside agencies allowed to search its cameras. It only ever shows the last thirty days. This ledger reads every Michigan portal it can once a day and keeps every copy, so the searches and changes the portals no longer show stay on the record.

Every figure here is counted from the portals’ own published files, captured daily for the agencies in the archive. Each portal shows about thirty days; the ledger keeps every capture. The portals themselves, and our archived copy of each, are linked on the Sources tab.

ONE WAY OR BOTH

Most of this access runs one way

A portal can publish two lists: who may search the department’s cameras, and whose cameras the department may search. Put side by side, they show that most of these relationships are not mutual. An outside agency can usually search the department’s cameras without the department being able to search its cameras in return.

PRIVATE CAMERAS

Some of the cameras police can search belong to businesses

Not every camera on this network is a government’s. Apartment communities, property managers and stores can buy Flock cameras too, and can let the police search them. The portals’ lists name those that do. It does not run the other way: no company can search the police cameras.

COMINGS AND GOINGS

Who was added, who was dropped

A live portal shows today’s lists and nothing else. Every addition and removal here comes from comparing one day’s read with the next, which is the only place these changes can be seen.

Organizations that left several portals at once

One portal dropping an organization is that department’s own decision. The same organization disappearing from several portals within two weeks is that organization leaving the network, which only reading every portal can show.

THE WHOLE NETWORK

The national network is shrinking, and the searches show it

A Flock customer’s cameras form one camera network, and a search can reach thousands of networks at once. Every search records how many it reached. The widest search run on a given day therefore measures how big the whole national pool was that day, and two departments with very different partners report nearly the same number.

1 · WHAT THE PORTALS SAY

How many cameras each department says it runs

This is the official figure: each portal states how many cameras the department operates. It never says where they are. The count can change over time, and this ledger records every change it sees.

2 · WHAT THE DEFLOCK MAP SHOWS

Where volunteers have found cameras

DeFlock is a volunteer project that maps licence plate cameras. Its map is built on OpenStreetMap, an open map anyone can edit, and this ledger reads that same data once a day. A volunteer can record a camera’s make and who owns it, but the owner is often left blank, and nothing here comes from the departments. Each dot is a mapped camera and each outline is the area a department polices. Pick a county to draw it alone, or a department to zoom to it.

3 · PUTTING THE TWO TOGETHER

Whose cameras are on the map

The portal counts the department’s own cameras. The map counts every Flock camera a volunteer has found inside the department’s area, whoever owns it. Setting one against the other gives a floor, never an exact figure. Where the map holds more than the portal claims, the extra cameras belong to someone else, such as a store, an apartment complex or a neighbouring department, or are no longer there. Where it holds fewer, some of the department’s cameras have not been mapped yet.

Cameras on the map with a business named as owner

Every mapped camera whose owner is recorded as a company rather than a police force or the vendor, and whether that company appears on any department’s list of cameras it can search.

4 · WHEN CAMERAS COME AND GO

Two different signals, kept apart

The portals and the map can both show a camera appearing or disappearing, and they mean different things. Only the portal’s count says a department actually added or removed a camera, and it never says which one. The map says where, but the date is when a volunteer edited the map, which can be months after a camera went up.

WHAT THEY FLAG

How often a passing car matches a hotlist

A hotlist is a list of plates the system alerts on, mostly stolen vehicles and outstanding warrants. Each portal publishes how many vehicles its cameras saw in the last thirty days and how many alerts they raised. One divided by the other is a rate that can be compared between departments, where the raw counts cannot.

        What only the ledger can tell you

        Reading a portal today gives you thirty days of searches and a sharing list with no history. The ledger keeps every earlier day as well, and the differences between them: organizations appearing and disappearing, figures moving, a read that failed and recovered hours later. None of that is visible on the live page.

        Caveats. A partner’s state is read from its name, since the portals publish no state field. Federal organizations are counted from the portal’s own [Federal] prefix. A county figure counts each organization once across the county’s departments, so county figures do not add up to the combined total. The first and last day of every trend line are partial days and are drawn faded. The “no crime named” grouping of search reasons is this ledger’s own, not a Flock or agency category.
